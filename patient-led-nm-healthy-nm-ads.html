Meta Ad Library Intelligence Brief

Two Groups, One Message, One Ad Budget Patient-Led NM and Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico in the HB 99 malpractice fight

Window: Aug 30, 2025 – Aug 30, 2026 Geography: New Mexico Retrieved: Aug 30, 2026

Executive Summary

Over the past year, patientlednm.org and healthynm.org ran what reads as a single, coordinated campaign to reshape New Mexico's medical malpractice law — but only one of them bought a single ad. Patient-Led NM spent $22,100–$31,450 across 50 Meta ads and 2.8–3.3 million impressions between September 2025 and March 2026. Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico spent $0. It has no ads of any kind — political or commercial — on either of its Facebook pages.

That division of labor is the story. Patient-Led NM carried the paid air war under a disclosed "paid for by" filing and a published list of four founding organizations. Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico carried the earned-media and research track — seven op-eds, a physician survey, and a statewide voter poll — while disclosing no funders, no staff, and no board. The two share a statistic ("248 physicians lost"), a villain (the trial bar), and a sourcing base: the survey CHNM commissioned was fielded to members of the Greater Albuquerque Medical Association and the New Mexico Medical Society, two of Patient-Led NM's four founders.

The reform side won. HB 99 passed the House 66–3 and the Senate 40–2 and was signed March 6, 2026. Patient-Led NM ran a one-week victory lap, then went dark on March 12, 2026 — and has not run an ad in the 171 days since. Its organized opposition, New Mexico Safety Over Profit, was outspent roughly 3.3 to 1 on Meta during the fight and is the only participant still advertising in New Mexico today.

The connective tissue

Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico's physician survey — fielded online November 11 to December 15, 2025, n=223 — drew its entire sample from members of the Greater Albuquerque Medical Association and the New Mexico Medical Society. Both are founding organizations of Patient-Led NM. The two groups therefore share a research base even though they present as independent entities and only one of them discloses who is behind it.

This is an inference about organizational proximity drawn from disclosed sourcing, not evidence of common control or common funding. No public filing reviewed for this brief establishes a legal or financial relationship between the two.

Scale check — is $31K a lot?

For a single-state legislative fight, yes. Patient-Led NM's 2.8–3.3 million impressions landed in a state of roughly 2.1 million people, so the campaign delivered on the order of 1.3 to 1.6 impressions per resident — enough for meaningful repetition against a target audience much smaller than the full population. (Impressions count ad views, not unique people; the same person seeing an ad ten times counts ten times.)

For context from this project's prior research: the entire field of GOTV advertisers in Texas spent $206K–$370K across a whole US Senate primary in a state 14 times New Mexico's size. A $22K–$31K single-issue buy inside one 30-day legislative session is a serious, well-resourced campaign at the state level — not a token presence.

Spend Against Events

Patient-Led NM's buy tracks the legislative calendar almost perfectly: a fall awareness build, a January surge timed to White Coat Day, a deliberate pause through the floor votes, and a March victory lap that ends the campaign.

The February gap is a tactic, not a retreat

Patient-Led NM launched zero new ads in February 2026 — the month HB 99 cleared the House 66–3, survived eleven Senate Judiciary amendments, and passed the Senate 40–2. That looks like abandonment until you check delivery: seven January ad objects worth $8,500–$11,193 kept running through February 20, including a single $3,500–$3,999 creative launched January 28, the group's largest single ad of the campaign.

The read: Patient-Led NM front-loaded its heaviest buy onto White Coat Day and let it run through the decisive votes rather than launching fresh creative into an active floor fight. Public pressure was applied before the votes; during them, the lobbying moved indoors.

The Framing War

Both sides ran testimony-driven campaigns built on individual New Mexicans harmed by the healthcare system. They disagreed entirely about who did the harming.

Word frequency in ad copy — Patient-Led NM vs. New Mexico Safety Over Profit
Top terms by raw frequency across each advertiser's ad text. NM Safety Over Profit's counts run higher because its analyzed corpus spans 313 lifetime ads against Patient-Led NM's 50.

Patient-Led NM's vocabulary is access and supply: patients (73), care (50), reform (36), access (33), doctors (29), malpractice (26). The problem is a shortage; the cause is a legal climate; the fix is legislative. Notably absent is any attack vocabulary — Patient-Led NM's paid copy never names trial lawyers as the villain, even though its allies do. That naming was left to the caucuses, ATRA, and the op-ed track.

New Mexico Safety Over Profit's vocabulary is accountability and harm: victims (181), justice (180), profits (158), corporations (153), robbing (144), greed (95). It never contests the doctor shortage directly in its highest-frequency terms; it reframes the bill as corporate liability relief. Its January 26 ad went further, citing an Albuquerque Journal report that "the debate over the number of practicing physicians is muddied by a lack of conclusive data" — an attack on the 248-physician statistic itself rather than on the policy.

Where and to Whom the Ads Landed

Patient-Led NM's delivery is tightly bounded to New Mexico. What spillover exists is consistent with border-market bleed, not with a multi-state strategy.

Relative share, normalized from Meta's per-ad average reach percentages. Meta reports these at one-decimal precision, so this is a coarse indicator of shape only — treat the gaps between adjacent bands as noise.

Delivery is not targeting

Everything above describes who saw the ads, not what the advertiser selected. Patient-Led NM's delivery skews toward the 65+ band, but that skew has at least four plausible causes beyond a demographic selector: an uploaded supporter or patient list whose composition drives delivery; Meta's optimization finding older users most responsive to healthcare-access creative; the resonance of physician-shortage messaging with heavy healthcare consumers; and placement effects, since Facebook's own user base skews older than Instagram's.

Gender delivery split essentially evenly (0.5% average reach each for female and male), which is itself weak evidence against tight demographic targeting. What the data supports: the campaign reached older New Mexicans disproportionately. What it does not support: any claim about whom Patient-Led NM set out to reach.

06

Strategic Assessment

1. The split-channel structure is the campaign's defining feature

Running a disclosed, "paid for by"-filed advertising vehicle alongside an undisclosed research-and-op-ed vehicle is a recognized advocacy structure, and it is efficient here for a specific reason. Meta's political-ad archive captures only what carries a disclaimer. By routing paid persuasion through Patient-Led NM — which names four founding organizations and files its disclaimers — the coalition accepts full transparency on the channel where transparency is mandatory anyway. Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico operates on channels with no disclosure regime at all: op-ed pages, commissioned polling, and owned media. The result is that the public record shows the transparent half of the effort and not the other half, and no rule was bent to produce that outcome.

2. Citizens for a Healthy NM was not on the same side of HB 99 as Patient-Led NM

This is the finding most likely to be missed. On February 2, 2026 — mid-session, with the bill in the House Judiciary Committee — Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico published "Latest on HB 99: Current Form Won't Stop the Bleed of Doctors from NM." It was attacking the bill for insufficiency while Patient-Led NM's paid campaign was pushing it toward passage and NM Safety Over Profit was attacking it from the opposite direction. All three positions coexisted in the same two weeks. Anyone treating "the reform side" as a single bloc will misread the endgame: the coalition that produced the win was not unanimous about whether the win was worth having.

3. The campaign was built to end, and it ended

Patient-Led NM's ad spend goes to zero eleven days after the governor's signature and stays there for 171 days. This is a legislative campaign in the strict sense — commissioned for one bill, one session, one outcome. There is no maintenance spend, no accountability program tracking implementation of the new damages caps, no early positioning for the 2027 session. Its website still carries a "Share Your Story" collection mechanism and a live events calendar, so the organization persists; the paid program does not.

4. Its opposition outlasted it

New Mexico Safety Over Profit was outspent roughly 3.3 to 1 during the fight and lost the vote 66–3 and 40–2. It is nonetheless the only participant in this brief still buying New Mexico ads, having pivoted after March to private-equity consolidation in nursing homes, utilities, childcare, and urgent care. Its December 2025 pre-session buy shows the same pattern in reverse: it was on Meta three weeks before the session opened. It is running a standing issue-advocacy program that treats HB 99 as one engagement; Patient-Led NM ran a campaign that treated HB 99 as the whole war.

5. Where the paid air war was not the decisive channel

HB 99 passed 66–3 and 40–2. Margins that lopsided are not produced by $31,000 of Facebook advertising. Reporting on the bill describes more than a year of negotiation and behind-the-scenes work ahead of the session, and the Senate Judiciary Committee alone adopted eleven amendments. The realistic function of Patient-Led NM's buy was to build a permission structure — visible public concern about doctor shortages, documented by a poll showing 91% of voters calling it a top priority — inside which legislators could vote for a bill the trial bar opposed. Judge it as air cover for an inside game, not as the thing that moved votes.

What to watch next

Does Patient-Led NM come back? If HB 99's damages caps face a constitutional challenge — a live possibility given the tiered structure and New Mexico's litigation history — the coalition has a built, disclaimer-ready vehicle sitting dormant. A restart would signal a defensive phase.

Does Citizens for a Healthy NM ever advertise? A first ad filing would force a "paid for by" disclosure and give the public its first look at the entity behind the op-eds and polling. Its continued absence from the archive is a choice, and it is the more informative outcome.

Does the fight follow the governor's race? Ken Miyagishima ran malpractice reform as a signature primary issue with $7,300–$22,649 in page-wide Meta spend across the window. Sam Bregman and Deb Haaland both engaged healthcare access. If reform is contested again in 2027, the vehicles are already built.

Methodology & Limitations

Data source. Meta Ad Library API, queried August 30, 2026. Window: August 30, 2025 – August 30, 2026. Country filter: United States. Advertisers resolved by Page ID before any page-scoped query.

How the zero was verified. Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico's absence was confirmed three ways: (1) the Ad Library API returns no queryable ad object for either Facebook Page linked from healthynm.org; (2) the Ad Library web interface, queried with all ad types and all statuses for the United States, returns "This advertiser isn't running ads"; (3) keyword searches on the organization's name, its "Policy" entity name, and its site URL return no matching ads. Its founding-adjacent organizations — the NM Hospital Association and NM Medical Society — likewise have no political-archive presence.

Clustering discipline. New Mexico Safety Over Profit's lifetime page total is $89,700–$131,687 across 313 ads dating to November 2024, but that corpus spans private equity, rideshare safety, and food safety alongside healthcare. The $5,500–$10,846 figure used throughout this brief is its December 2025 – March 2026 healthcare cluster only. The American Tort Reform Association's figure is likewise its New Mexico creative only, isolated from a multi-state buy. Attributing full page totals to a single issue would have overstated both by an order of magnitude.

Known limitations.

  • Spend is a range, never a number. Meta reports bands ($100–$199, $1,000–$1,499). Midpoints are used only for relative comparison and charting; every headline figure in this brief is stated as its full range.
  • The archive captures only disclaimer-filed ads. Commercial ads, organic posts, and boosted content without a "paid for by" filing are invisible here. This is precisely why the $0 finding for Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico should be read as "no archived Meta advertising," not "no advertising anywhere." Google, connected TV, radio, print, and direct mail are entirely out of scope and were not measured.
  • Delivery ≠ targeting. All demographic and geographic figures describe who saw an ad, not what the advertiser selected. See the callout in Section 5.
  • Demographic precision is low. Meta returns per-ad average reach percentages at one decimal place, which for a 50-ad campaign compresses the age distribution into a handful of distinguishable values. The age chart shows shape, not magnitude.
  • Monthly bars count new ad launches. An ad launched in January and delivering through February is booked to January. February 2026 shows zero new launches for Patient-Led NM but $8,500–$11,193 of January inventory still in delivery.
  • Funding is not established. Neither organization's donors are documented in this brief. Patient-Led NM discloses four founding organizations; Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico discloses none. No inference about common funding or control between them is supported by the evidence gathered here.
