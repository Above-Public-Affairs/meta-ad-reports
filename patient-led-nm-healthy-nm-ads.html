Window: Aug 30, 2025 – Aug 30, 2026 Geography: New Mexico Retrieved: Aug 30, 2026

Executive Summary

Over the past year, Patient-Led NM (patientlednm.org) and Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico (healthynm.org) ran a sustained campaign to change New Mexico's medical malpractice law. Both argue the same case in nearly the same words: New Mexico is losing physicians faster than any other state, liability costs are the reason, and the legislature must act. Together they put $22,100–$31,450 of Meta advertising, 2.8–3.3 million impressions, seven published op-eds, two commissioned research instruments, a statewide summit series, and a Roundhouse mobilization day behind that case.

The two operate on separate channels. Patient-Led NM carried all of the paid media — 50 disclaimer-filed Meta ads between September 2025 and March 2026, backed by four named founding organizations: the New Mexico Medical Society, the New Mexico Hospital Association, the Sacramento Mountains Foundation, and the Greater Albuquerque Medical Association. Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico carried the research and earned-media track — op-ed placement, a statewide voter poll, and a 223-physician survey — and ran no advertising at all, on Meta or any archived channel, while disclosing no funders, staff, or board.

The campaign achieved its objective. House Bill 99 passed the House 66–3 and the Senate 40–2, and Governor Lujan Grisham signed it on March 6, 2026, creating tiered punitive-damages caps and raising the evidentiary standard for punitive claims. Patient-Led NM ran a one-week victory lap and placed its final ad on March 12, 2026. Neither organization has published or advertised since — this was a campaign built for one bill and one session, and it ended when the bill was signed.

Shared research base

Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico's physician survey drew its entire 223-respondent sample from members of the Greater Albuquerque Medical Association and the New Mexico Medical Society — two of Patient-Led NM's four founding organizations. The two campaigns therefore rest on a common evidentiary base, and the "248 physicians lost" statistic appears verbatim across both.

This is an observation about organizational proximity drawn from disclosed sourcing. No public filing reviewed for this brief establishes common control, common funding, or any legal relationship between the two entities.

Campaign Cadence

Read as one operation, the campaign has a clean four-phase shape: an op-ed opening in late summer, a paid awareness build through the fall, a research-and-advertising surge into the session, and a short victory lap.

The February gap is a tactic, not a retreat

Patient-Led NM launched zero new ads in February 2026 — the month HB 99 cleared the House 66–3, survived eleven Senate Judiciary amendments, and passed the Senate 40–2. That looks like abandonment until you check delivery: seven January ad objects worth $8,500–$11,193 kept running through February 20, including a single $3,500–$3,999 creative launched January 28, the campaign's largest.

The read: the buy was front-loaded onto White Coat Day and left to run through the decisive votes rather than launching fresh creative into an active floor fight. Public pressure was applied before the votes; during them, the work moved indoors.

Most frequent terms in Patient-Led NM ad copy
Raw frequency across all 50 ads. The vocabulary is consistently access-and-supply oriented; notably, the paid copy never names trial lawyers or the plaintiffs' bar as an adversary.

The absence is as informative as the presence. Patient-Led NM's own advertising builds an access case — patients (73), care (50), reform (36), access (33), doctors (29) — and stops there. It does not assign blame to litigators, insurers, or hospitals. That naming was left to other actors in the debate: the House Republican caucus ran "trial lawyers have spent years pouring money into New Mexico politics," and the American Tort Reform Association ran a "lawsuit abuse" cost frame. Keeping the coalition's own paid voice free of adversary language is a deliberate positioning choice for an organization whose credibility rests on being patient-led rather than provider-funded.

The Research and Earned-Media Program

Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico produced the numbers the campaign argued from. Both instruments were released without naming the firm that conducted them.

InstrumentField datesSampleSelected findings
Statewide voter poll Jan 20–22 Likely NM voters, text + phone 79% concerned they won't see a doctor in time · 89% believe there is a doctor shortage · 84% support malpractice reform including punitive caps · 91% call it a top legislative priority · 84% say lawmakers who are trial lawyers should recuse
Physician survey Nov 11 – Dec 15, 2025 n=223 (181 practicing, 40 retired, 2 departed) via GAMA + NM Medical Society 34% likely to leave the state · 50% likely to retire early · 96% say too few specialists for proper standards of care · 91% call NM unfavorable for private practice

The site's supporting case is built on liability economics rather than testimony: New Mexico carries the nation's highest medical malpractice loss ratio, with insurers paying roughly $183 in claims per $100 collected in premiums against a national average near $74; malpractice suits run at over twice the national rate; and only 24–40% of physicians trained in the state remain to practice there. It attributes the shift to House Bill 75 (2021), which raised damage limits and expanded liability.

A campaign that pressed for more than it got

Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico's final published item, on February 2, 2026, was not an endorsement. Titled "Latest on HB 99: Current Form Won't Stop the Bleed of Doctors from NM," it argued the amended bill was insufficient to reverse physician departures — published while the bill sat in House Judiciary and while Patient-Led NM's paid campaign was driving toward passage.

The bill that emerged carried eleven Senate Judiciary amendments and a tiered cap structure that treats large hospital systems very differently from independent providers. Read together, the two tracks show a campaign that won its vote while one of its own vehicles was on record saying the win was incomplete — useful context for anyone assessing whether this coalition considers the matter closed.

Where and to Whom the Advertising Landed

Delivery is tightly bounded to New Mexico. What spillover exists is consistent with border-market bleed, not with a multi-state strategy.

Regional delivery — number of Patient-Led NM ads reaching each state
All 50 ads reached New Mexico at 0.9% average reach share. Neighboring states appear on a minority of ads at negligible reach, the expected signature of metro-boundary spillover around El Paso, the Four Corners, and the Colorado line.
Age distribution of ad delivery
Relative share, normalized from Meta's per-ad average reach percentages. Meta reports these at one-decimal precision, so this is a coarse indicator of shape only — treat gaps between adjacent bands as noise.

Delivery is not targeting

Everything above describes who saw the ads, not what the advertiser selected. Delivery skews toward the 65+ band, but that skew has at least four plausible causes beyond a demographic selector: an uploaded supporter or patient list whose composition drives delivery; Meta's optimization finding older users most responsive to healthcare-access creative; the resonance of physician-shortage messaging with heavy healthcare consumers; and placement effects, since Facebook's user base skews older than Instagram's.

Gender delivery split essentially evenly — 0.5% average reach each for female and male — which is itself weak evidence against tight demographic targeting. What the data supports: the campaign reached older New Mexicans disproportionately. What it does not support: any claim about whom Patient-Led NM set out to reach.

Scale check — is $31K a lot?

For a single-state legislative fight, yes. The campaign's 2.8–3.3 million impressions landed in a state of roughly 2.1 million people, delivering on the order of 1.3 to 1.6 impressions per resident — enough for meaningful repetition against a target audience much smaller than the full population. (Impressions count ad views, not unique people.)

For context from this project's prior research: the entire field of GOTV advertisers in Texas spent $206K–$370K across a full US Senate primary in a state fourteen times New Mexico's size. A $22K–$31K single-issue buy inside one 30-day legislative session is a serious, well-resourced state-level campaign — not a token presence.

07

Assessment

1
The two-vehicle structure concentrates disclosure where disclosure is mandatory
Meta's political archive captures only what carries a "paid for by" filing. Routing paid persuasion through Patient-Led NM — which names four founding organizations and files disclaimers — puts full transparency on the one channel where it is compulsory anyway. Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico operates on channels with no disclosure regime: op-ed pages, commissioned polling, owned media. The public record therefore documents the funded, attributable half of the effort in detail and the other half not at all. Both are ordinary advocacy practice; the asymmetry is a feature of where disclosure rules apply, not evidence of impropriety.
2
The campaign was built to end, and it ended
Paid spend goes to zero eleven days after the governor's signature and stays there for 171 days; the research track's last publication was February 2. This is a legislative campaign in the strict sense — one bill, one session, one outcome. There is no maintenance spend, no accountability program tracking implementation of the new damages caps, and no early positioning for 2027. Both websites remain live with active collection mechanisms, so the organizations persist even though the programs do not.
3
The paid air war was not the decisive channel
HB 99 passed 66–3 and 40–2. Margins that lopsided are not produced by $31,000 of Facebook advertising. Reporting describes more than a year of negotiation ahead of the session, and Senate Judiciary alone adopted eleven amendments. The realistic function of the buy was to build a permission structure — visible public concern about doctor shortages, documented by a poll showing 91% of voters calling it a top priority — inside which legislators could vote for a bill the plaintiffs' bar opposed. Judge it as air cover for an inside game, not as the thing that moved votes.
4
The organized opposition outlasted the campaign
New Mexico Safety Over Profit spent roughly a third as much during the fight and lost the vote decisively, yet it is the only participant still buying New Mexico ads — having pivoted after March to private-equity consolidation in nursing homes, utilities, childcare, and urgent care. Its December pre-session buy shows the same discipline in reverse: it was on Meta three weeks before the session opened. It runs a standing issue-advocacy program that treated HB 99 as one engagement; this campaign treated HB 99 as the whole war.
5
The coalition does not consider the matter settled
Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico went on record mid-session saying the bill in its amended form "won't stop the bleed." That position was never retracted, and the tiered cap structure that drew the objection is what became law. Anyone modeling the 2027 session should assume the reform coalition has a documented, unresolved grievance with the statute it just helped pass — and two built, dormant vehicles available to act on it.

What to watch next

Does the paid program restart? If HB 99's damages caps face a constitutional challenge — plausible given the tiered structure and New Mexico's litigation history — Patient-Led NM is a built, disclaimer-ready vehicle sitting dormant. A restart would signal a defensive phase.

Does Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico ever advertise? A first ad filing would compel a "paid for by" disclosure and give the public its first look at the entity behind the op-eds and polling. Its continued absence from the archive is a choice, and the more informative outcome.

Does the issue follow the governor's race? Ken Miyagishima ran malpractice reform as a signature primary issue with $7,300–$22,649 in page-wide Meta spend across the window, and both Sam Bregman and Deb Haaland engaged healthcare access. If reform is contested again in 2027, the vehicles are already built.

Methodology & Limitations

Data source. Meta Ad Library API, queried August 30, 2026. Window: August 30, 2025 – August 30, 2026. Country filter: United States. Advertisers resolved by Page ID before any page-scoped query. Organizational and research details drawn from patientlednm.org and healthynm.org as published.

On the absence of advertising by Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico. Verified three ways: the Ad Library API returns no queryable ad object for either Facebook Page linked from healthynm.org; the Ad Library web interface, queried with all ad types and all statuses for the United States, returns "This advertiser isn't running ads"; and keyword searches on the organization's name, its "Policy" entity name, and its site URL return no matching ads. Its founder-adjacent organizations — the NM Hospital Association and NM Medical Society — likewise have no political-archive presence, so no ad buying is being routed through them.

Clustering discipline. New Mexico Safety Over Profit's lifetime page total is $89,700–$131,687 across 313 ads dating to November 2024, but that corpus spans private equity, rideshare safety, and food safety alongside healthcare. The $5,500–$10,846 figure used throughout is its December 2025 – March 2026 healthcare cluster only. The American Tort Reform Association's figure is likewise its New Mexico creative isolated from a multi-state buy. Attributing full page totals to a single issue would have overstated both by roughly an order of magnitude.

Known limitations.

  • Spend is a range, never a number. Meta reports bands ($100–$199, $1,000–$1,499). Midpoints are used only for relative comparison and charting; every headline figure is stated as its full range.
  • The archive captures only disclaimer-filed ads. Commercial ads, organic posts, and boosted content without a "paid for by" filing are invisible here. The $0 finding should be read as "no archived Meta advertising," not "no advertising anywhere." Google, connected TV, radio, print, and direct mail are out of scope and were not measured.
  • Delivery ≠ targeting. All demographic and geographic figures describe who saw an ad, not what the advertiser selected. See Section 5.
  • Demographic precision is low. Meta returns per-ad average reach percentages at one decimal place, which for a 50-ad campaign compresses the age distribution into a handful of distinguishable values. The age chart shows shape, not magnitude.
  • Monthly bars count new ad launches. An ad launched in January and delivering through February is booked to January. February 2026 shows zero new launches but $8,500–$11,193 of January inventory still in delivery.
  • Poll and survey figures are self-reported. Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico does not name the firm that conducted either instrument, and neither full crosstabs nor margin of error is published. Its physician survey is a voluntary sample of two medical-association memberships, not a random sample of New Mexico physicians.
  • Funding is not established. Neither organization's donors are documented here. Patient-Led NM discloses four founding organizations; Citizens for a Healthy New Mexico discloses none. No inference about common funding or control between them is supported by the evidence gathered.
